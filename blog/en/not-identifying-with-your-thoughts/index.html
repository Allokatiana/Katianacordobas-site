What Does It Really Mean Not to Identify With Your Thoughts? And Why Is It So Hard?

Most of our suffering doesn't come from what happens to us, but from the thoughts we automatically believe about what happens.

This may sound simple at first. Many spiritual teachings repeat it in different ways: don't identify with your thoughts. Yet when we try to live it in real life, we quickly realize it is not so easy.

For about fourteen years now, I have been paying closer and closer attention to something most of us rarely stop to examine: my own thoughts. And over time, my way of exploring them has changed in ways I didn't expect.

First, Trying to Change My Thoughts

At the beginning, I believed the solution was to change my thoughts. If a thought made me feel bad, I assumed I needed to replace it with a better, more positive one that would bring a different emotion. It seemed logical: if negative thoughts produce negative feelings, positive thoughts should produce better ones.

Sometimes this worked, at least for a while. But after some time I saw its limits. Even when I replaced one thought with another, I was still inside the same effort of constantly managing and controlling what was happening in my mind. The mind remained the center of the struggle.

Then, Trying to Understand Them

Later my approach changed. Instead of replacing my thoughts, I began trying to understand them. When a thought appeared, I asked: Why is this thought here? What belief might be behind it? What is it trying to do?

These questions helped me see patterns I hadn't noticed before. Certain beliefs, fears and expectations were shaping how I interpreted situations, and understanding my thoughts let me recognize many unconscious patterns that had been driving my emotional reactions for years. This stage was extremely valuable. It brought awareness to many things that had been invisible to me.

A New Practice: Observing Without Explaining Everything

Over time, my approach kept evolving. I didn't stop questioning my thoughts or trying to understand them; I still do sometimes, and it can be very useful. But I also realized something important: if I tried to analyze every thought that appeared, the mind could keep me busy forever. There would always be another thought to interpret, another explanation to look for, another pattern to investigate.

So alongside understanding, I began another practice: simply observing. Sometimes, when a thought appears, instead of analyzing it right away, I just notice it. I notice that it appeared, I notice the sensation or emotion that may come with it, and I let it be there without necessarily explaining it. Through this simple observation, something interesting began to become clear.

Thoughts Appear on Their Own

Little by little, I saw that thoughts were appearing by themselves. They weren't always something I was consciously choosing. A moment of silence could suddenly be followed by a thought, and even when I tried to quiet the mind, another thought would eventually arrive.

This led to a simple but important realization: if thoughts appear by themselves, without being consciously chosen, then perhaps they are not the same as the one who is observing them. In other words, our thoughts may not be who we are.

Why This Is So Hard to Live

Understanding this intellectually is one thing; experiencing it in daily life is another. Thoughts can be extremely convincing. A thought often produces emotions and sensations in the body, such as fear, tension, excitement or sadness, and the body reacts almost immediately. Because the body reacts so strongly, the thought begins to feel completely real. So the question becomes very practical: how can I see a thought as just a thought when it creates such strong sensations in my body?

The Invisible Habit of Believing Our Thoughts

Recently I started reading Falling into Grace by Adyashanti. At the beginning of the book, he speaks about thoughts and suffering in a remarkably simple and clear way. He points to something that happens in all of us, all the time: thoughts appear in the mind, and we take them as reality without even noticing that we are doing it.

For years I had heard spiritual teachers say, "Don't identify with your thoughts." I understood the sentence intellectually, but I didn't truly understand what it meant in experience. The difficulty wasn't that I consciously believed every thought was true. It was simpler than that: most of the time, I wasn't questioning them at all. Thoughts were happening, and I was simply thinking them.

Thinking Happens Like Breathing

Thinking is a little like breathing. We breathe all the time, but most of the time we aren't aware of it. Thoughts move constantly through the mind in the same way, and we rarely notice them as something separate from ourselves.

When we begin to observe them more closely, something becomes clearer. Thoughts appear, stay for a moment and then disappear, much like sounds moving through the air. Imagine sitting quietly and suddenly hearing a car pass, a bird sing or a door close somewhere. You didn't create that sound, and you can't control exactly when it comes. It simply happens.

Thoughts are very similar. They arise, stay a moment and pass. The problem is not that thoughts appear; the mind naturally produces them. Just as the lungs breathe, the mind thinks.

When Thoughts Create Suffering

The difficulty is that we often treat thoughts as if they were reality itself. A thought appears, and we immediately assume it says something true about the world, the future or ourselves. We rarely pause to ask whether it is actually true, or simply a thought passing through.

This is also why suffering can be so hard to release. People sometimes say, "Just let it go," but it rarely feels that simple. Suffering is often built on thoughts we believe without realizing it, and as long as the mind is convinced they are true, the suffering feels justified and inevitable.

Imagine someone loses their job. That can already be a difficult situation. But what often increases the suffering are the thoughts that follow: that they will never find another job, that their life is ruined, that they aren't good enough, that nothing will work out. These thoughts can feel extremely convincing. The body reacts with anxiety and tension, and the situation starts to feel overwhelming. But if we look carefully, we may notice something important: these are thoughts about the future, not facts. They are possibilities imagined by the mind.

The Loop Between Thoughts and the Body

Through meditation and observation, I noticed something else. Thoughts often create emotions and physical sensations, and those sensations make the thoughts feel even more real. It becomes a loop: a thought appears, the body reacts, and that reaction reinforces the feeling that the thought must be true. Once the loop begins, it becomes very easy to believe the thought without questioning it.

The Practice of Observing

The practice is not about rejecting thoughts or forcing the mind to stop. It isn't about declaring a thought wrong or saying it shouldn't exist, because that kind of reaction usually produces more thoughts and more resistance.

The practice can be much simpler. We observe the thought. We notice when it appears, the emotion or sensation that comes with it, and how quickly the mind wants to believe it. And slowly, something becomes clearer.

Waves on the Ocean

Thoughts are like sounds, images or movements appearing in our experience. They arise and they pass. Many spiritual teachings use images to describe this. Some compare thoughts to clouds moving across the sky: the clouds move and change, but the sky remains.

The image that speaks to me most is the ocean. Waves are constantly moving on the surface, but the ocean is much larger than those movements. In this sense, thoughts are like waves, and awareness is like the ocean itself. The waves rise and fall, and the ocean remains.

This is one way to understand what spiritual teachings mean when they say we are not our thoughts. Thoughts are movements happening in the mind; they come and go. What we are may be the awareness that notices them, the quiet presence in which thoughts, emotions, sensations and experiences appear and disappear.

A Practice That Takes Time

Recognizing this isn't always easy, because thoughts trigger emotional and physical reactions that make them feel so convincing. That is why this understanding usually develops slowly. It doesn't come only through intellectual knowledge; it grows through experience, by observing the mind again and again. Little by little we begin to see how thoughts arise, how they influence our emotions, and how easily we assume they must be true. Some people recognize this quickly, while others need time to explore it, and that is completely normal.

So the invitation is simple: keep observing your thoughts. Don't fight them, and don't judge them as good or bad, since even judging a thought is just another thought appearing. Just notice them.

Slowly, something begins to change. The suffering those thoughts create can start to loosen, the mind becomes quieter, and little by little a sense of space and freedom may appear. A thought appearing in your mind doesn't mean you have to believe it, and sometimes that simple discovery can change the way we experience our whole inner world.
