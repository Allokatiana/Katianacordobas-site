The "Shoulds" That Govern Us and the Illusion of Perfection

We live in a world woven with shoulds: I should be patient. I should be a good mother. I should always be there for others. I should meditate more, eat better, be in great shape.

These shoulds whisper to us constantly, shaping our actions, our emotions and even our sense of worth. They guide us and give us direction, and often they lead us toward growth. These conditionings aren't good or bad in themselves. There is beauty in being kind, responsible, present or disciplined, and many of these shoulds bring us joy, purpose and fulfillment.

Where we get lost is that we don't simply hold these shoulds as possibilities. Without realizing it, we create an ideal version of ourselves, one that should always meet these expectations perfectly.

Where This Ideal Comes From

And where does this ideal version come from? From the child within us.

For a child, being loved means being safe. When we were young, we watched the world around us and learned what was praised, what was accepted and what made us feel secure. In that innocence, we made choices, unconscious and deeply rooted ones. We decided that if we were always kind, we would be loved; that if we succeeded, we would be safe; that if we never disappointed anyone, we would belong.

We created an inner map of how to exist in the world, believing that as long as we followed it, we would be protected. And because no one told us otherwise, we mistook that map for our identity.

But perfection isn't fixed. It is fluid, always changing, different for each person and impossible to define with the human mind. It is an abstract, shifting idea that we can never fully grasp. And yet we spend our lives trying to meet an impossible standard created by a much younger version of ourselves, out of fear, out of longing, out of the simple and natural need to be loved.

When We Fall Short

We cling to these shoulds so tightly that we believe our happiness, our worth, and even our ability to be loved depend on meeting them. And because this perfection doesn't actually exist, we live in constant self-judgment.

When we inevitably fall short, when we lose our patience, skip a workout or need space instead of always being there for others, what happens? Instead of allowing ourselves to be human, we reject ourselves. We feel we are not enough. And if we're not enough, we fear losing the love of others; we may feel unsafe, anxious, or as if something terrible were about to happen.

The most subtle trap is this: because we believe so strongly in these shoulds, we stop seeing them. We don't recognize them as something we adopted; we believe they are us. We become blind to the fact that they are conditioned thoughts, mental constructions, and in that blindness we stay caught in the shadows of our own mind, always trying to measure up to an illusion we can never quite reach.

Simply Noticing

But what if we simply noticed these shoulds? Not as something to fix or something bad, just as something to see. Because when we see them clearly, something profound happens: we realize we are not them.

If you can observe your shoulds, then who is the one observing? Are these shoulds truly you, or are they echoes of conditioning, beliefs handed to you by your upbringing, your culture, your religion, your school, and the way your parents, siblings and friends treated you? Are they patterns formed by experiences, wounds or survival mechanisms that once kept you safe?

Again, conditioning itself isn't the enemy. Being a good mother is a beautiful and noble conditioning; it can be one of the highest expressions of love. But when we cling to it so tightly that we reject ourselves for not always meeting our own impossible expectations, we trap ourselves in a cycle of self-judgment without realizing it. We confuse being a good mother with an unreachable perfection, and when we inevitably fall short, we suffer.

Others as Mirrors

And it doesn't stop there. We extend this rejection to others too. Just as we judge ourselves, we judge the people around us who don't meet the same shoulds. We project our expectations onto them and feel frustrated when they don't match what we think they should be.

But others aren't obstacles to our growth; they are mirrors. They reflect what we are, what we want and what we don't want. They show us the very patterns that shape us, not so we can judge them, but so we can see them.

The real work isn't about eliminating our conditioning. It is about observing how we react when we fall short, and noticing how attached we are to these shoulds, as if our peace and happiness depended entirely on meeting them.

To Be Loved Is to Be Love

And yet there is a truth waiting to be uncovered: to be loved is to be love. The moment we truly realize this, everything changes.

This realization can't be forced. It isn't about fighting the layers of conditioning or tearing them away. It comes through gentle observation, curiosity and allowing. We peel back the layers not as a struggle, but as a dance of discovery. Each time we see more clearly, we remember who we truly are beneath the conditioning, and we realize that our essence was never lost, only covered by old wounds, thoughts and reactions.

This process continues again and again. It isn't a one-time revelation, but something we live in this moment, in deep presence. It is about being with what is, without resistance, and when resistance does arise, not resisting the resistance, simply observing it.

The Path of Surrender

Transformation doesn't happen by force. It isn't our strength that brings light into the shadows; it is the Divine that transforms, in its own time and its own way. God does the healing. We co-create with the Divine not through effort, but through surrender.

When we surrender, we align with divine intelligence and with the great unfolding of life, and in that alignment we experience something far greater than the expectations of our shoulds: something infinitely vast, infinitely loving and infinitely free.

This is the path: not fixing or judging, but seeing; not struggling, but surrendering; not seeking love, but realizing that we are love. And every time we remember this, we come home to ourselves.
