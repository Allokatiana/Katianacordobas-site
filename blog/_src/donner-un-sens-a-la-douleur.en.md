# Making Sense of Emotional Pain

There are moments when suffering doesn't shatter us but wakes us up, when we stop fearing pain as a failure or a fall and begin to recognize it as a threshold: an opening toward coherence, and toward the memory of something vast that sings even through the shadow.

Recently, I found myself in one of those moments. A song opened the door: "Je suis malade," sung with raw anguish by Lara Fabian. It wasn't just a song of heartbreak; it felt like a testimony to the sacredness of sorrow. I felt her pain not as a weight, but as light filtered through the ache. And suddenly my own memories of loss, grief and uncertainty began to shimmer, not as wounds, but as reflections of something noble.

I remembered a moment beyond this world: an ascent, carried by spiraling lights of violet and white, until I arrived in the stillness of love itself. Not a person and not a place, but a state, infinite and unconditional. From there I saw creation, and within it pain and joy, light and shadow. All of it was breathtaking, even anxiety, even fear. It was all part of the same tapestry.

Pain is not an error in the system. It is the pressure that invites us to reorganize ourselves, a temporary collapse from which a new coherence is born.

This is not a solitary idea. Mystics and philosophers have been saying the same thing for centuries. Rumi wrote that the light enters us through our wounds. The Kabbalists speak of the shattering of the vessels and the sacred work of repairing them. Buddhism teaches that suffering, *dukkha*, is not a curse but a doorway to liberation. And Christian mystics like Saint John of the Cross wrote of the dark night of the soul as the very path to union with God.

To me, these are all reflections of the same truth: light and shadow are not enemies, but notes of the same music. Shadow is not the opposite of light; it is what helps us see it more clearly.

So I wrote, because this feeling, this knowing, was not meant to stay inside me.

This is not about glorifying suffering. It is about redeeming it: seeing from above how the darkest valleys reflect the highest peaks, and how the ache of a lost love reveals the immensity of love itself.

I believe this is why we came here: to feel it all, to remember the beauty hidden in the folds of sorrow, and to walk the spiral of human experience until we understand that pain is not exile, but invitation. And when we truly see it, we realize it was never separate from the light. It was the echo of the light, on its way home.

Katiana
