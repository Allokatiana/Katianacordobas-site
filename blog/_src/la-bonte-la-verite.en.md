# Kindness, Truth and the Courage to Be Real

*Reflections on the phrase "It is better to be kind than to be right"*

You may have heard the phrase "It is better to be kind than to be right." It is simple, wise and undeniably beautiful. But like many beautiful things, it holds a deeper truth that is often overlooked: sometimes, in our desire to be kind, we end up abandoning what is true.

We silence ourselves. We nod when we should speak, we smile when something inside hurts, and we agree, not because we truly agree, but because we're afraid that disagreeing will make others uncomfortable or disappointed. That isn't kindness; it is erasing ourselves, and it is very different from love.

Real kindness is not the absence of truth. Real kindness includes truth.

When we think kindness means making ourselves smaller to make room for others, we aren't being kind; we are being afraid. Afraid of not being liked, accepted or seen as good. So we fold ourselves up to keep the peace and avoid tension, and in doing so, we neglect our own heart.

This is especially true for those of us who see ourselves as "good people": helpers, healers, givers. We often confuse being kind with saying yes to everything, avoiding conflict and keeping everyone comfortable, even if it costs us our voice.

### Love Begins With You

If you can't express your truth, your needs, your boundaries or your disappointments, something essential is being denied. And when you deny your truth long enough, it doesn't just go away. It turns inward and becomes resentment and self-abandonment. Eventually, it also tends to attract people who do the same: who dismiss your needs, cross your boundaries and don't hear you, because without realizing it, you have shown them how.

That is why we need to be careful with the phrase "it's better to be kind than to be right." Sometimes, doing the right thing *is* the kind thing.

Imagine you buy something, and when you go to pick it up, it isn't what you ordered. You have two options. You can say nothing and take it, because you don't want to make anyone uncomfortable. Or you can say, calmly and clearly, "This isn't what I paid for." Both can be done kindly, but only one honors the truth.

It isn't about being harsh, aggressive or confrontational. You don't need to be unkind to be true. In fact, the most powerful kindness often comes through honesty, through clear and respectful self-expression. Authenticity isn't the opposite of kindness; it is its foundation.

### Knowing When to Speak and When to Be Silent

And yet, wisdom also means knowing when to speak and when to stay silent. There are moments when insisting on being "right" serves no purpose, when the other person isn't open, or when the truth would only inflame things rather than heal them. In those moments, silence can be a sacred act: not a way of suppressing yourself, but an act of discernment. You aren't silencing yourself; you are choosing peace, because peace serves everyone.

Again, the key is awareness. We can ask ourselves: Am I staying quiet out of love, or out of fear? Am I speaking to bring clarity, or to feel superior? Does my kindness come from strength, or from a hidden sense of not being worthy?

When we express ourselves from a grounded, loving place, we honor both truth and kindness, and we stop treating them as opposites. There is a middle path, a balanced and conscious space where kindness flows from truth rather than away from it, where we speak not to win or to be "right," but to be real: to be aligned, and to love ourselves and others without abandoning either.

So perhaps the real teaching isn't to choose between being kind and being right, but to choose what is real, and to express it with kindness. Truth without love is harsh, and love without truth is hollow. But truth expressed with love heals. That is what lifts us, and that is what sets us free.
